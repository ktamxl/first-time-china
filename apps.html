Chapter 01 · Apps

The app stack

Six apps do almost everything. Install them at home, on wifi, before you fly — because the moment you need them most is the moment you have no data.

Checked November 2026

The rule that saves the first day

Install everything before you leave. Chinese apps are generally available in overseas app stores, but downloading them after you land means relying on airport wifi, a local number you may not have yet, and an app store that may not refresh. Do it at home, sign in once, and confirm each app opens without a VPN.

The trap

Some apps ask for a Chinese phone number at registration. Register with your home number where allowed, and add the Chinese number later if you buy a local SIM. If an app insists on a Chinese number, that is a signal to use the web version or ask the hotel — not to abandon the plan.

The setup order

  1. Data first. Activate the eSIM or buy the SIM, and confirm the phone has internet without wifi. Everything below needs a connection. Data & power →
  2. WeChat account. Register, set a password you will remember, and turn on two-factor. Add your passport in the payment section even if you will mostly use Alipay — some services only accept WeChat.
  3. Alipay, verified. Add the same Visa or Mastercard, complete identity verification with your passport, and make one small payment before you fly if the app allows a test. Money →
  4. Trip.com. Create the account, then add every traveller — passports and all — before you book anything. This is the step people leave too late. Trains →
  5. DiDi. Sign in with a phone number, add a payment method, and set your home address in English and Chinese.
  6. Dianping and Amap. Install, and set the city you are visiting. Both are mostly Chinese; the translation routine below is how you use them.

WeChat: more than messages

WeChat is where a Chinese business expects to talk to you. A hotel will send check-in details through it, a restaurant will take a booking through it, a hospital will issue an appointment through it. Treat it as infrastructure, not chat.

Official accounts 公众号

These are the business pages inside WeChat. To find one: open the search bar, type the company name in Chinese or English, and look for the result with a verified tick and the company logo. Follow it and look along the bottom menu for words like 预订 (reserve), 服务 (service) or 会员 (membership).

This is how you reach a hotel's front desk, a restaurant's booking line, or a hospital's appointment system without speaking Chinese on the phone. If a company has no official account, the hotel concierge is the next best route — see reservations.

Mini programs 小程序

Apps that live inside WeChat and need no installation: metro tickets, museum bookings, queue numbers, bike rental, power-bank rental. You reach them by scanning a QR code or searching inside WeChat. They are usually Chinese-only, so keep the translation routine handy.

Family group chat

For a group of six or eight travelling together, a WeChat group is more practical than international SMS: it is free over data, shares locations, and translates messages in place. Create the group before the trip, add everyone by QR code, and pin it. Then agree on one rule: meet-up changes go in the group, not in a direct message, so nobody is out of the loop.

Why not just call?

Voice calls over data are fine, but in a noisy station or a restaurant, text plus a shared location is faster and leaves a record. Locals will often reply by voice message rather than typing — that is normal, and WeChat will transcribe some of it.

Using Chinese-only apps

Dianping and Meituan have no English interface and are unlikely to get one. The workaround is mechanical, and once learned it takes seconds:

  1. Use the app's own structure, not its words. In Dianping, the second tab from the left is restaurants; the tab with a crown or flame is the ranking list. You can navigate by position and icon without reading a single character.
  2. Screenshot, then translate. Both iOS and Google Translate will translate text inside a photo. Point the camera at a menu, or translate the screenshot afterwards.
  3. Copy the Chinese name into a note. The single most useful habit on this trip: keep a note with the Chinese name and address of every place you plan to visit, so you can show it to a driver or a passer-by.
  4. Use WeChat's built-in translation. Long-press a received message and choose translate; it handles most day-to-day Chinese.
Offline matters

Download the offline language pack and screenshot the addresses before you leave the hotel. Signal inside stations, basements and older buildings is unreliable, and that is exactly where you will need the words.

Trip.com's planner

Trip.com includes an itinerary planner that builds a day-by-day plan for a city or a whole route, with travel times between stops. It is genuinely useful for the part most people get wrong — how much fits in a day — and you can use it without booking anything through them.

The practical combination: plan in Trip.com, book the pieces you want there (trains, transfers, eSIM, attraction tickets) because they land in one itinerary, and book hotels wherever the rate is best. The planner is a planning tool, not a contract.

One caution

Planner suggestions are generic. Treat the timings as a starting point, then cut a third of them — the plan will still be too full for a family with children.

Apps worth knowing about, later

  • PonyPilot+ 小马智行 — robotaxi hailing in parts of Shenzhen. See Getting around.
  • Octopus 八達通 — the Hong Kong transit card, and it lives in Apple Wallet.
  • MTR Mobile — Hong Kong metro routes and journey times.
  • Airline apps — for seat changes and boarding passes when the airport wifi is struggling.

Resist installing twenty apps. Six working ones beat twenty half-configured ones, and every extra app is another password to recover at a bad moment.
