Chapter 04 · Data & power

Data, eSIMs and power

Everything in this guide depends on a connected phone. Sort the data before you fly, and bring a plan for charging that does not involve the battery in your suitcase.

The one technical fact worth understanding

Traffic on a foreign eSIM or on home roaming is routed outside mainland China, so services that are blocked locally — Google, WhatsApp, Instagram — usually keep working. A mainland SIM routes traffic locally, so they do not. This single difference decides which option you want.

Sharing data with the family

The simplest method is a personal hotspot from one phone. It works, it is free, and the only cost is battery — which is why the power bank section below matters.

  • One phone as the hub. Choose the person least likely to wander off, and set a hotspot name and password the children can remember.
  • Turn the hotspot off when walking. It drains a phone roughly twice as fast as normal use, and a dead hub means a family with no maps.
  • Second phone as backup. Buy a small second data plan, or keep one phone on home roaming. Do not let the whole family depend on a single battery.
  • Download before you leave the hotel. Maps, addresses, tickets and this guide should all be readable offline.

What is blocked, and what to do

On a mainland connection, Google services, WhatsApp, Instagram, Facebook and most Western news sites are unreachable. Apple Maps, iMessage and email generally work. This is a fact of the network, not a fault in your phone.

Practical approach: arrive with a foreign eSIM or roaming if you want those services, and otherwise switch to the local equivalents — Amap for maps, WeChat for messages, Dianping for reviews. Do not depend on a VPN for anything urgent: availability is unpredictable, and banking or payment flows are a bad place to test it.

VPN: when to use one, and when to switch it off

A VPN is useful for a narrow job: reaching Google, Gmail, WhatsApp, Instagram or a Western news site while you are on a mainland connection. It is not a way of life, and it is not something to leave running.

Turn it off before you pay

Payment apps run risk checks on the network you are connecting from. A proxy can make a normal payment look suspicious, and the failure is confusing: the app simply declines. If a payment fails while a VPN is on, switch it off and try again before assuming the card is the problem.

  • On demand, not always-on: enable it, check mail or post the photograph, then disable it. Battery and reliability both improve.
  • Do not use one for banking or anything involving a verification code. The risk checks are stricter and the failure mode is worse.
  • Download the app before you fly. VPN providers' sites are not reliably reachable from inside China, and app stores may not serve them.
  • Expect it to be unreliable. Availability changes week to week; never build a day around needing one.

After a few days the pattern inverts. Maps, payments, trains, food and messages all live in Chinese apps that work perfectly without a VPN, and the Western services stop mattering. The visitors who struggle are the ones who keep fighting the network instead of switching to the local tools — open the VPN when you need it, close it when you are done, and let the phone become a Chinese phone for the week.

A word on rules

Consumer VPN use sits in a legal grey area in China, and enforcement varies. Businesses use approved international lines. Nothing here is advice to break the law — it is how visitors commonly handle a practical problem, and you should make your own call.

Power banks: the rule that surprises people

Since mid-2025, Chinese aviation security requires power banks carried on flights to have a 3C certification mark and a capacity of 100Wh or less. Power banks without the mark — including many bought in Europe or America — are routinely confiscated at airport security, and there is no argument to be had at the checkpoint.

Do not rely on your own battery

If your power bank has no 3C mark, assume it will not fly. Either leave it at home or accept losing it. The alternative is better than the original: rent one.

Renting instead

China runs on shared power banks. They sit in cabinets in convenience stores, restaurants, malls and metro stations, rented through WeChat or Alipay mini programs — usually a small deposit, then a few yuan per hour, capped per day. Scan the QR on the cabinet, take a bank, and return it to any cabinet of the same brand when you are done.

  • Return it before you leave the city. The deposit and the bill follow the account, and returning in another city is not always possible.
  • Check the cabinet has a free slot before you take one out — otherwise you cannot return it.
  • Carry a cable. The banks come with the battery, not always the cable you need.

Plugs and voltage

Mainland China uses 220V, 50Hz, with type A (two flat pins), type C (two round) and type I (three slanted) sockets. Hong Kong uses the British type G. A universal adapter covers everything; most hotels have USB outlets beside the bed, but not always enough for a family.

One useful item: a small multi-port charger with two USB-C ports. It replaces three wall chargers and one argument about whose phone is charging.

The small kit that earns its place

  • Tissue pack — many public toilets have no paper. This is not a joke.
  • Hand sanitiser — useful, and often missing in washrooms.
  • A copy of your passport photo page, on paper and in photos.
  • A card with your hotel's name and address in Chinese, in every pocket.
  • One universal adapter and one multi-port charger.
  • A foldable shopping bag — plastic bags are chargeable and sometimes absent.
