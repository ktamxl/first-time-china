Chapter 06 · With kids

Travelling with children

Three generations in one trip works if the day is built around one anchor, one meal, and one rest. Everything else is optional.

Checked November 2026

Pacing

The failure mode is not a bad attraction; it is a schedule with four of them. Chinese cities are large, the walking is real, and the heat in summer is genuinely punishing. Build each day around one anchor — a park, a museum, a neighbourhood — and let the rest be negotiable.

  • Morning is the reliable window. Children are at their best, queues are shortest, and the light is kinder.
  • Plan a midday retreat. Back to the hotel, or a long lunch in air conditioning. This is normal local practice, not a concession.
  • One big meal out per day. The other meal can be noodles, dumplings or room service.
  • Alternate days. A big day, then a light one. Three big days in a row breaks the grandparents before it breaks the children.

Finding a restroom

This is the most-asked practical question, and the answer is a short list of reliable places:

WhereHow goodNotes
Shopping mallsBestClean, free, usually with a baby room. Every large mall has them, on every floor.
Metro stationsUsually goodNot every station, and sometimes outside the fare gates — ask staff.
Convenience storesHit and missSome have a toilet for customers; ask.
HotelsReliableWalk in and use the lobby facilities. Nobody will stop you.
Parks and streetsVariablePublic toilets exist but quality varies. Amap lists them.

Two habits: carry tissue, because many toilets do not supply it, and expect squat toilets outside malls and hotels. Teach children the stance before the trip rather than in the moment.

In Amap 高德地图, search 公共厕所 — public toilet — and it will show the nearest ones on the map.

Metro and attraction tickets

Children under roughly 1.2–1.3 metres ride the metro free, through the wide staffed gate. Above that, buy a child ticket at a machine or the service desk — you cannot tap them through on your phone. The height is measured at the gate, so do not buy in advance.

For attractions, book on Trip.com or the venue's WeChat mini program. Many museums now require a timed reservation with a passport number, and popular ones sell out days ahead in school holidays. Book the anchor, not the whole day.

Food and water

  • Bottled water for drinking; tap water is not for drinking anywhere on the mainland. Hotels provide kettles and bottled water.
  • Hot food, peeled fruit, familiar staples. Noodles, rice, dumplings, steamed buns and roast meats are safe bets for cautious eaters.
  • Mild by default. Ask for 不要辣 and order one plain dish per meal — steamed egg, greens, plain rice.
  • Hong Kong is easier: tap water is drinkable and the food safety standard is familiar.

If someone gets sick

For anything minor, a pharmacy 药店 is on most streets and the staff will help if you show symptoms in translation. For anything serious, go to an international clinic in Shanghai or Shenzhen, or a private hospital in Hong Kong — they have English-speaking staff and take international insurance. Ask your hotel to call ahead; walk-in registration at a public hospital is a long process in Chinese.

Before you go

Check that your travel insurance covers mainland China specifically — some policies exclude it — and save the insurer's assistance number offline, not just in your email.

The family kit

  • Tissue packs and hand sanitiser — one per bag.
  • A refillable water bottle per child, filled from hotel-supplied water.
  • Snacks from home for the train and the queue.
  • A light folding stroller if the child is under four; lifts exist but are not everywhere.
  • Copies of every passport, on paper, in a bag that stays with an adult.
  • A written card with the hotel address in Chinese, in each adult's pocket.
