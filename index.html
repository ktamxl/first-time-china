Your first trip to China,
in the right order.

This is not a list of attractions. Written from a family trip with three generations: grandparents, parents, and small children.

Why the order matters

Almost every bad first day in China is the same story: the phone has no data, the payment app was never verified, and the taxi driver cannot read the address. None of those are hard problems. They are sequencing problems, and they are all solved before you board the plane.

So this guide is built as a chain. Each link assumes the one before it works. If you do only the first four, you will land in better shape than most first-time visitors.

Who this is written for

Families and couples on a 10-day to two-week trip, usually two or three generations travelling together. Many readers are Western-born Chinese going back for the first time, travelling with parents who remember the country and children who have never seen it.

That shapes everything. A guide for backpackers can say “just figure it out”. A guide for a family with a six-year-old and a grandmother cannot. So this one assumes luggage, naps, queues, and a fixed number of hours in a day.

It also assumes you do not read Chinese. Every name in this guide is given in characters you can show to someone — Simplified on the mainland, Traditional in Hong Kong.

What this guide is not

  • Not a ranking of attractions. Cities get one good day and a few decisions, not a top-forty list.
  • Not a restaurant database. That lives at Ken's Table, and this guide links to it rather than duplicating it.
  • Not neutral about politics or behaviour. It explains why things work the way they do, in the first person, from what was observed. It does not tell you what Chinese people are “like”.
  • Not permanent. Anything that changes quickly is dated. Assume a page older than a year needs re-checking.

A note on dates

China updates faster than any printed guide can follow: visa lists, app rules, booking windows and payment limits all move within months. Every page here carries the month it was last checked, and the changelog records what changed.

Where a detail is likely to drift — a price, an opening hour, a queue — the page gives a range and tells you to confirm on the day. Where a detail is stable — how a banquet is seated, why nobody tips — it is written plainly.
