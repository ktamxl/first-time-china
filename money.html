Chapter 02 · Money

Two wallets, one card

China runs on QR codes. Set up both Alipay and WeChat Pay before you fly, link the same card to each, and treat one as the backup for the other.

Checked November 2026

Why two apps, not one

Alipay and WeChat Pay overlap almost completely, which is exactly why having both is worthwhile. Either can fail for reasons that have nothing to do with your account: a merchant only accepts one of them, a card network hiccups, a verification step expires, or a driver is using a personal QR code that behaves differently from a business one.

From experience

Paying a Shenzhen taxi fare through Alipay failed twice. The same card, through WeChat Pay, worked immediately. The likely reason is that some drivers receive money into a personal Alipay account rather than a merchant account, and the foreign-card route does not always clear that way. The lesson is not “Alipay is bad” — it is never travel with only one payment method.

Setting them up

  1. Download both apps at home and register with your normal phone number.
  2. Add the card. In Alipay: Me → Bank Cards. In WeChat: Me → Services → Wallet → Bank Cards. A Visa or Mastercard in your own name is the simplest; a debit card sometimes avoids foreign-transaction friction.
  3. Verify your identity with your passport. Both apps will ask for a photo of the passport and sometimes a selfie. Do this at home, on wifi, with good light — not in an airport queue.
  4. Test it. Buy something small before you travel if the app allows it, or make your first purchase at the airport where there is help nearby.
  5. Set a payment PIN you will remember, and enable biometric unlock if offered.
Fees and limits move

Foreign-card transactions may carry a fee above a threshold, and that threshold changes. Check the fee screen inside the app before a large payment, and do not assume the rules you read last year still apply.

Where it works — and where it doesn't

SituationWhat to do
Convenience store, restaurant, mallScan the merchant's QR, or show your payment code. Both apps work.
Metro and busA transport QR inside Alipay, or a physical transit card. See metro.
Street vendor, small shopPersonal QR codes are common. Small amounts usually work; if one app fails, try the other.
TaxiDiDi in-app payment is easiest. For street taxis, scan the driver's QR — and keep the second app ready.
Hotel depositA physical card is often requested for the hold, even if you pay by QR.
Anything officialPassport plus cash or card. Do not assume a QR code is accepted for government or medical payments.

Cash still works — carry some

Carry ¥300–500 in small notes, plus a little Hong Kong dollars for the Hong Kong leg. Cash is accepted everywhere by law, and it is the fallback when a phone dies, a QR code will not scan, or a card is declined. ATMs at banks and airports accept foreign cards; hotels can usually change money but at a poor rate.

Two practical notes: many small merchants have no change for a ¥100 note, so break them early; and a torn or heavily marked note may be refused.

Palm payment

WeChat Pay offers a palm-payment system — you enrol your palm once at a kiosk, then pay by holding your hand over a reader. It exists at selected metro gates, convenience stores and office buildings, and it is genuinely convenient if you live there.

Do not plan around it

Enrolment generally requires a Chinese identity document and a Chinese mobile number. For a visitor on a foreign passport it is often unavailable, and coverage is limited to specific locations. Treat it as a curiosity you might encounter, not a payment strategy.

Children and money

Children do not need their own wallet. You pay for everything, and a child's metro ticket can be bought at a machine or at the staffed gate — details in travelling with children.

What is worth doing: give each older child a small amount of cash in a pocket, and teach them to show the Chinese address note if separated. A physical note in a pocket works when a phone is dead.

Tipping: don't

Tipping is not customary and can cause confusion — in restaurants, taxis, and hotels. The price is the price. The exception is Hong Kong, where small change in taxis and a service charge in restaurants are normal; check the bill, since many Hong Kong restaurants add 10% automatically.
