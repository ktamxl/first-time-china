Chapter 03 · Getting around

Getting around

Three cities, four modes: metro for the city, DiDi for the last mile, rail for the long jumps, and a cross-border van when you have luggage and children.

Official taxi rank, always

Ignore anyone approaching you inside the terminal offering a car. Follow the signs to the official taxi queue, or order a DiDi to the designated pickup point. The licensed queue is cheaper and regulated.

Pre-booked pickup is the calmest arrival with children: a hotel car, a Trip.com airport transfer, or a cross-border van if you are heading straight to another city. The driver meets you inside arrivals with a name board, which removes the taxi queue, the address problem and the luggage problem in one move. Book it a few days ahead and give the flight number.

The metro

Every city here has a metro that is clean, cheap, signed in English, and faster than a car in traffic. Three ways to pay:

MethodHowBest for
Single ticketBuy at a machine — English button top right — tap in, tap out, keep the token.One-off journeys; children without phones.
QR codeAlipay → Transport, or WeChat → Services → Transport. Scan at the gate.Everyone with a phone. Works in all three cities with the right city selected.
Transit cardShanghai card, Shenzhen Tong 深圳通, Octopus 八達通. Buy at station service desks.Families, repeated journeys, children.

Cards in your phone

Hong Kong's Octopus can be added to Apple Wallet and topped up from a foreign card — the easiest transit experience on the trip. Shanghai's public transport card can also be added to Apple Wallet; Shenzhen is simplest with a QR code or a physical card. On Android, do not assume wallet support for Chinese transit cards — use the QR route instead.

Children on the metro

Children below roughly 1.2–1.3 metres usually ride free, admitted through the wide staffed gate. Above that height they need a child ticket, which you buy at a machine or from the service desk. The rule varies by city and is measured at the gate, so do not pre-buy.

If you have a stroller, look for the lift symbol on station maps before you enter — not every exit has one, and carrying a stroller up a long staircase in a crowd is the single most tiring thing you will do all day.

DiDi 滴滴出行

DiDi is the local equivalent of Uber, and the international version has an English interface and accepts foreign cards. The flow is the same as at home — set the pin, confirm the car, watch it arrive — with three local differences:

  • The driver may call. You will not understand the call. Send a message in the app instead; the app offers canned phrases and translates replies.
  • Check the plate, not the model. Match the licence plate on the app to the car at the kerb before you get in.
  • Pickup points are specific. At malls and stations, the app names a gate or a level. Walk to it — a driver will not circle for you.

For a family, choose the larger vehicle class when it is offered. Four adults plus two children does not fit in a standard car with luggage, whatever the app suggests.

Robotaxis in Shenzhen

Parts of Shenzhen — notably Nanshan, around Houhai and the bay — run a public robotaxi service operated by Pony.ai, booked through its own app 小马智行. The cars drive themselves within a geofenced area, and the app shows you whether your pickup and drop-off are inside it before you book.

  • Register before you rely on it. Sign-up may ask for a Chinese mobile number and identity verification; do this while you still have time to fall back.
  • Check the geofence. Airport runs and cross-district trips are usually outside it. Treat it as a city service, not a transfer service.
  • Have DiDi ready. If no car is available, or the route leaves the zone, the app will simply find nothing.
Worth doing once

For children — and adults — a driverless car is the memorable half-hour of a Shenzhen day. Book it deliberately, not as transport you depend on.

Trains, booked on Trip.com

China's high-speed rail is the best way between cities: 350 km/h, on time, and far more comfortable than flying for the distances on this itinerary. Trip.com sells the same seats as the official railway, in English, with a small booking fee.

Add every traveller first

This is the step that catches families. Before booking, open Profile → Travellers and add each person separately, with:

  • Full name exactly as printed in the passport — surname and given names in the right boxes. A middle name omitted or added is a mismatch at the gate.
  • Passport number, nationality, date of birth, expiry.
  • A separate profile for each child, including infants. A child cannot travel on an adult's profile.

Do this at home, for all eight people, before any booking. Changing a traveller's passport details after a ticket is issued is not a simple edit — it usually means a refund and a rebook.

Booking and boarding

  1. Search by station, not city. Shanghai has several; the high-speed trains leave from Hongqiao 上海虹桥. Shenzhen's main long-distance station is Shenzhen North 深圳北.
  2. Choose a class. Second class is 3+2 seating and perfectly comfortable; first is 2+2 and quieter; business is a different product entirely. For a family with children, first class is often worth the modest premium.
  3. Pay and keep the confirmation. The ticket is electronic and tied to the passport — there is nothing to collect in most cases.
  4. Arrive 45–60 minutes early. Big stations are airports: security, queues, and long walks to the platform. The gate closes a few minutes before departure.
  5. Use the passport at the gate. If the automatic gate rejects it, go to the staffed lane beside it — this is routine for foreign passports, not a problem with your ticket.
Luggage

There is no strict weight limit on high-speed trains, but racks are overhead and at the carriage ends. Two large cases per family is manageable; four is not. Ship luggage ahead if you are moving between cities with a lot of bags.

Booking a cross-border van

Book through the hotel concierge, a Hong Kong travel agency, or Trip.com's cross-border car service. Give them: pickup address, drop-off address, date and time, number of passengers, luggage count, and every passenger's name as printed in the passport. Expect roughly HK$700–1,500 per vehicle depending on route and time; the driver handles the vehicle paperwork, you handle your own immigration.

Why it is worth it with children

The van removes two taxi rides, a station walk, and a queue with suitcases. On a day when you are changing cities, that is several hours of your life back — and the child car seat question is easier to arrange in advance.
